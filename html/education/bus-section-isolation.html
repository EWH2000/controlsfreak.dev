Bus Section Isolation BACnet

Part of an MS/TP trunk is down. Where do you go first? That is the only question on this page. The answer is an order of attack, and it starts with the pattern: what is offline, set against the floor plan, before anything else. BACnet MS/TP covers why a trunk fails — the token ring, the addressing, the two wires. This page covers finding the spot, with a made-up floor to work three jobs on.

{# Owner's account, first person, lightly recast from his own words. Every clause traces to what he said; nothing is added. Do not add a reason, a reading, or any detail of the job. (A Nunjucks comment, so it stays out of the built page.) #}

True story. I was going around all day. If I had just studied the floor plan and what was offline, and gone that route first, I could have found the break in the bus way faster. The bigger lesson was to not get bogged down with the likes of trying to use a DMM on a bus and theorizing based on just those numbers, and to focus instead on more apparent problems, such as all of the rooms in one area being out.

The order of attack

First, try to find a bus layout, and see whether an obvious spot shows on it. On a daisy chain a single break takes out every controller past it, so on a drawn route the dead section starts at the break.

No layout? Look at the MAC addresses of the offline devices or, better, their room numbers, and start at the highest or the lowest. It is not a coin toss between the two: everything past a single break is dead, so one end of the trunk is fully offline, and the break is at the other edge of the dead run, the one next to devices still online. That is the same spot a layout would have shown.

Whole trunk flaky? Break it at the midpoint and see whether the half nearer the supervisor or router becomes stable, since sometimes a device can be transmitting when it should not and take others down.

What the made-up floor leaves out

The floor in the game below is made up, and it is simpler than a real one. It carries one fault per job, where a real trunk can carry more than one at once. Its break only cuts rooms off, where a real break also leaves the still-connected section without the terminator that sat at the far end. And its trunk runs in room-number order, with the MAC addresses handed out the same way: that order is a given of this made-up floor, and the job with no layout depends on it. Termination is in the two wires, and reading a symptom back to its layer is in reading the symptom, both in the MS/TP lesson.

Work the floor

One made-up floor: sixteen zone controllers in rooms 201 to 216, around a core that holds the stairs, the elevator, the restrooms and an electrical room. All sixteen are on one daisy-chained MS/TP trunk that starts at the supervisor or router, in the electrical room. The trunk runs in room-number order, and the MAC addresses were handed out the same way, MAC 1 in room 201 through MAC 16 in room 216. The room numbers run right around the floor, so 201 and 216 are next to each other on the plan, but the trunk does not join them: it leaves the electrical room into one of the two and ends in the other, and which way round is part of each job. What you see is what the supervisor or router reports: each controller online, offline, or flaky (dropping in and out).

Every job has a single fault: a break in the trunk, or one controller transmitting when it should not (the troublemaker). Every move is the same gesture, picking a controller on the plan, and the score is how many stops or splits it took. New job deals a fresh fault of the same kind.

{# The three calls. Static markup; the script only toggles [hidden]. Each one says what a pick DOES on that job, which is a rule of the made-up floor, not field practice. #}

Job 1 · A dead section, and you have the bus layout

A made-up call: a run of rooms has dropped offline, and there is a bus layout. The trunk route is drawn on the plan, starting at the supervisor or router. Pick the controller where you would stop first. A stop shows the trunk on both sides of that controller, as far as the next one each way — live (still tied to the supervisor or router), dead (cut off from it), or broken — and it finds the break if the break is on either side.

Job 2 · A dead section, no layout

A made-up call: a run of rooms is offline again, and nobody has a layout. The route is not drawn until you find the break, and the call does not say which way the trunk leaves the electrical room, into 201 or into 216. What you have is the floor plan and the offline list: room numbers and MAC addresses. Pick the controller where you would stop first. A stop works as before: it shows the trunk on both sides of that controller, and it finds the break if the break is on either side.

Job 3 · The whole trunk is flaky

A made-up call: every controller on the trunk is dropping in and out. Picking a controller splits the trunk on its far side: that controller and everything nearer the supervisor or router are connected, and everything past it is cut off. Only one split is open at a time: a new pick rejoins the trunk and splits it at the new spot. The plan then shows whether the connected part is stable or flaky. Stable means the troublemaker is in the part cut off; flaky means it is in the connected part. Keep splitting until one controller is left that may hold the fault. On this made-up floor, where one controller is the whole cause, splitting what may still hold the fault at its midpoint halves it each time: sixteen controllers, four splits.

What this page didn't cover

This page stops at finding the section. What to check once you are standing at the spot is a different question, and the three layers a fault could belong to — the token ring, the addressing, the two wires — are walked in BACnet MS/TP. Meter work on the bus is left out on purpose: the question here is where to go first, and the answer is the pattern.
