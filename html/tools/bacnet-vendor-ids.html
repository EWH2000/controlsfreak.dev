What a BACnet vendor ID is

A 16-bit number ASHRAE assigns to a manufacturer once and never reassigns, exposed as the Device object's Vendor_Identifier (property 120). Every device a company ships reports the same number — it identifies the maker, not the unit. Registration is free, which is why the list is long and full of names that never turn up on a job site.

The registry's first two rows are its origin story: ID 0 is ASHRAE itself, and ID 1 is NIST, which organized and hosted the earliest BACnet interoperability testing. Everything after that is the industry arriving roughly in the order it adopted BACnet.

Where you see vendor IDs in the field

Three places, usually: the I-Am a device answers during Who-Is discovery (a workstation's discovery log lists it next to the device instance), the Device object's property sheet when you pull up property 120, and the decoded I-Am in a Wireshark capture. In all three it's the same bare number — which is exactly when you want this table.

It also namespaces the proprietary parts of the protocol. Object types 128–1023 and the high property numbers are vendor-specific — see the ranges on the BACnet Object Reference — so "whose device is this" is the first question when a capture shows an object type the standard doesn't define. The vendor ID answers it.

Reserved and never-assigned vendor IDs

Seven numbers — 555, 666, 777, 888, 911, 999, and 1111 — are held by ASHRAE and will never be assigned to a vendor. A device or a capture reporting one of them is running a placeholder, a test stack, or a misconfiguration, not a product from a real manufacturer.

Other holes in the sequence are registrations that were withdrawn or numbers that were never issued; because IDs are never reassigned, they stay empty forever. And since ASHRAE issues new IDs continually, a number above this snapshot's ceiling usually just means the device is newer than the table — not that it's bogus.
