Simulators · AHU Depiction Mockup

Air-handler depiction — round 2

A depiction mockup for review, not a working simulator. Every value on this page is static: there is no physics, no clock and no interaction beyond the component drill-downs and the sensor glyphs, which light the annotation they feed when you hover or tab to them. This is a two-round record. Round 2 is the current proposal and it is drawn from the owner's own production graphics; round 1's three compositions are kept below it, unchanged, because the composition question they settled is not the only thing worth looking at twice.

Round 2 — variant A composition, real-graphic conventions

leader callouts · component colour

Composition is variant A's, settled in round 1 and untouched: one proportional casing elevation, stations strictly in air-path order, return dropping into the cabinet top, fan after both coils. Four things changed, and they all came out of the same source — the DDC graphics the owner builds professionally.

Controlled components are colour-coded. A device the program reads or writes carries an identity colour — dampers amber, the fan green, the heating coil warm, the cooling coil blue — so a device is found before a word is read. A component with no point on it stays neutral line-art, which is why the fixed intake louver is grey standing right beside the amber outside-air damper: the drawing itself says which one the program can move. The colour says what a device is, never what it is doing, so an idle heating coil is still fully warm. Colour is a supplement though, never the only channel: every component is also drawn as the thing it is, which is why the cooling coil carries the refrigerant distributor feeding its circuits and the heating coil carries a two-way valve on its pipes. Turn the colour off and the drawing still reads.

Leader lines replaced the badge band. Round 1 put every value in one reserved horizontal strip in air-path order, and that strip is what pinned all three layouts — two of the three converged because nothing else carried attachment. A titled callout box tied to its device by a leader line carries attachment on its own, so a value can sit anywhere there is empty space. That is also why the canvas grew: with leaders, the space below the unit became usable, and the three devices inside the cabinet are annotated from there.

Setpoints and measurements read differently. Every value sits in a bordered well, and the well's ink says where the number came from: plain bright for something a sensor measures, green for something the program commands or holds, blue in a blue-framed well for something calculated from other numbers on the screen. Booleans carry a state dot inside the well, and a false one goes dim.

The values that belong to no device moved to a grouped rail. Setpoints, the economizer lockout and the minimum outside-air position are the sequence's constants — they belong to the program, not to a box in the picture, so a leader line pointing at anything would be a lie. They are grouped by function rather than by location, and the rail is deliberately not a hand-control region.

Reading this screen

What this screen is. The supervisory graphic for a single-zone air handler — the screen a technician opens to see what the unit is doing right now. Air runs left to right: outdoor air at the intake, return air down from the zone, the two streams blending in the mixing box, then the filter, the heating coil, the cooling coil and the supply fan.

How to read it. Every device the program can read or write carries its own colour — dampers amber, the fan green, the heating coil warm, the cooling coil blue — so you can find a device before you read a word. Colour is never the only channel, though: each device is also drawn as the thing it is, which is why the cooling coil carries the small wedge-shaped refrigerant distributor and feeder tubes that no hydronic coil has, and the heating coil carries a two-way valve on the pipes below it. A component drawn in plain grey has no point on it. Values sit in bordered wells tied to their device by a leader line: a green value is something the program is commanding, a plain value is something a sensor is measuring, and a blue value is calculated from the others.

What the sequence is doing. The unit has a cooling call, and the 74.0 °F space reading is not on its own the reason for it: the space rose past the 75.0 °F make point, the stage latched on there, and it stays made until the space falls back to the 73.0 °F setpoint. So 74.0 falling from above 75.0 still calls, where 74.0 rising from below 73.0 would not. Outdoor air is 62.0 °F — below the 65.0 °F economizer lockout — so the program has driven the outside-air damper wide open and closed the return damper. Every cubic foot of supply air is outdoor air, which is why the mixed-air temperature reads the same as the outdoor-air temperature. Free cooling alone is not closing the gap, so the first stage of mechanical cooling has picked up the rest and the air leaves 7.0 °F colder than it arrived, at a 55.0 °F discharge. Read the ΔT well: it is discharge minus mixed air — the fan sits downstream of both coils on a draw-through unit, so that span covers the fan as well — and it goes negative whenever the unit is removing heat. When a unit stops doing its job, that is the number that collapses toward zero. The sequence itself is worked through on the economizers lesson.

Setpoints and the deadband. The separation between the two setpoints and the deadband are easy to read as one number, and they are not. The cooling and heating setpoints sit 5.0 °F apart — 73.0 °F and 68.0 °F — and the SP DIFF well is that separation, 73.0 minus 68.0. Nothing sets it: move either setpoint and it follows, which is why it is inked blue like the ΔT well rather than the green its two neighbours carry. The 2.0 °F deadband is the smaller, separate thing, and it belongs to whichever setpoint is currently active rather than to the pair. On the cooling side it sits above the setpoint: the stage makes at 73.0 plus 2.0, or 75.0 °F, and breaks back at 73.0 °F. Here the setpoint is the cut-out, so the zone rides the band above the number on the screen and a degree of drift cannot short-cycle the compressor. That is also why a space of 74.0 °F can sit under a lit stage — between the two edges the call is held by a latch, not by a comparison against the setpoint.

The operating state, in numbers

The unit is a single-zone constant-volume air handler with an economizer, a hot-water heating coil, a two-stage DX cooling coil and a draw-through supply fan. It is economizing at full outside air, and free cooling alone is not meeting the load, so the first DX stage has picked up the remainder. All four drawings on this page show this one state — the third column describes how round 2 reads it; round 1 carries the same values in its badge band.

Variant B — schematic DDC graphic

two blocks · three annotation registers

Deliberately less proportional than the machine. The economizer section is its own block, joined to the casing by a mixed-air duct run, and the annotation is split into three horizontal registers that never cross: measured temperatures in the badge band, commanded outputs in a rail below it, component names under the casing.
